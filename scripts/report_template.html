Can you replace Wealthfront S&P 500 Direct with Robinhood's MCP?

Yes, technically. Robinhood's agent MCP exposes tax lots, quotes, order history and order placement, which is everything a direct-indexing engine needs. The open-source design below backtests well. The catch is the money: the fee you'd save is small next to the tax-loss-harvesting value you'd be re-implementing, and a bug costs more than the fee.

Bottom line

  • DIY is feasible. Robinhood's agent MCP has every primitive a direct-indexing engine needs: get_equity_tax_lots, get_equity_quotes, get_equity_orders (across all your Robinhood accounts), review_equity_order and place_equity_order with dollar-based fractional buys.
  • The fee saving is small. At 0.09% the saving is {{FEE_100K}} a year per $100k. Across start dates from 2016 to 2022, the backtested tax value of harvesting was {{RATIO_LO}}–{{RATIO_HI}}× the Wealthfront fee over the same period. The harvesting is what makes direct indexing worth it, and Wealthfront already does it for you.
  • When DIY makes sense: large balances where 0.09% becomes thousands a year, or wanting custom exclusions or tilts, or a harvest trigger Wealthfront won't expose. It also makes sense if you want to own and understand the machinery. If none of that applies, consider keeping Wealthfront for the core and running this repo on a small agentic account first.
  • Two unknowns decide how good DIY can be. First, whether place_equity_order accepts specific tax lots. Second, what the MCP rate limits are. A 500-name portfolio makes {{F_TRADES}} trades a year in this backtest.

What Robinhood's MCP gives you

Robinhood launched its agentic-trading MCP server in beta on May 27, 2026. It is hosted at https://agent.robinhood.com/mcp/trading and you sign in with OAuth. The agent can read all your Robinhood accounts but can only trade in a dedicated "Robinhood MCP" individual account, funded with whatever you move into it. Trade approvals are off by default for external agents. Turn them on and every order needs a tap in the app.

For comparison, Wealthfront S&P 500 Direct charges 0.09% with a $5,000 minimum. Ways to compute the mix for a $X portfolio

Three construction methods are implemented. Each turns index weights into a holdable portfolio for a given account size. Live weights come from SPY's daily holdings file (float-adjusted, per share class). The chart shows ex-ante tracking error from a 15-factor statistical risk model fit to one year of daily returns, as of {{STATIC_DATE}}.

  • Full replication with fractional shares: every constituent at cap weight, dropping positions under a minimum dollar size. This is the default.
  • Optimized sampling: hold N names and solve a quadratic program to minimize tracking error against all ~500. With 200 names the ex-ante TE is {{TE_OPT200}}; with 100 it is {{TE_OPT100}}.
  • Top-N by size: simple but tilted toward mega-caps. Top 100 runs {{TE_TOP100}} TE, so avoid it.

The harvesting and rebalancing design

Each trading day the engine runs two phases: sells, then buys sized from the cash those sells free up.

  1. Harvest. Find any lot at least 5% below its basis with a loss of at least 1bp of the portfolio ($10 at $100k). Skip the name if it was bought in the last 30 days in any reported account, unless the whole position is being sold. Deepest losses go first.
  2. Replace. Move the sold weight to the 3 stocks whose daily returns correlated most with it over the past year. Each replacement can absorb at most 0.1–0.3% of the portfolio, and a harvest only happens if the replacements have room. An optional S&P ETF "sleeve" can take the overflow instead. Across four start dates it harvested slightly more but tracked worse, so it is off by default. The sold name is blocked from purchase for 31 days.
  3. Trim drift, tax-aware. Overweights are sold only from lots at a loss or with a gain of 0.5% or less. Long-term gain lots are sold only when look-through active share exceeds 10%, cheapest tax first. Short-term gains are never sold to rebalance.
  4. Deploy cash into the largest underweights. Orders under $1 or 0.05% of the portfolio are skipped.
What broke along the way, and why it matters for anyone building this. The first version had four problems, all now fixed and covered by tests.
  • In the March 2020 crash nearly every stock crossed the threshold at once. Harvested weight cascaded onto the few names still buyable, and the portfolio lost 10% in 2020 while the index gained 18%.
  • Harvesting only a stock's recent losing lots set its whole target to zero. The engine then sold its old, highly appreciated lots to "rebalance."
  • Replacement capacity scaled with each name's weight, so NVDA, AAPL and MSFT soaked up harvested weight and were later trimmed at large long-term gains.
  • Rebalancing tightly back to the index realized more long-term gains than harvesting saved. A loose drift budget was better after tax.
  • A fixed $10 minimum loss meant $5k–$25k accounts almost never harvested. The minimum now scales with the account.
  • Optimized and top-N portfolios swapped names at every re-solve and picked replacements outside the held set, which forced gain-realizing sells. Selection is now sticky and replacements come from the target set.

Read the knob table with care. Harvesting is path-dependent: one different sale changes which lots exist for the next ten years. Re-running with tiny code changes moved these dollar figures by about ±$5k per $100k. Treat smaller differences between rows as noise. Three patterns held in every run:

  • A tight 3% drift budget realizes long-term gains that cancel most of the harvesting. Budgets of 10–20% did best.
  • Whole-position (FIFO-safe) harvesting is worth less than lot-level harvesting, because a position's older, appreciated lots block the sale.
  • Optimized portfolios realize far more long-term gains than full replication, because each quarterly re-solve shifts weights between held names. Use them only if order count forces you to.

Proposed open-source repo

The repo design rule: the LLM is a courier, not a portfolio manager. A deterministic, unit-tested Python engine decides every trade. The agent, or a headless MCP client, only moves data in and orders out, and every order goes through review_equity_order first.

Daily loop (agent mode)

  1. Claude Code with robinhood-trading connected builds snapshot.json. The snapshot holds tax lots, cash, quotes for ~503 names, and 31 days of orders across all Robinhood accounts. It also includes your external_buys.json for Wealthfront, 401k and IRA purchases.
  2. ddi plan --phase sells writes a hashed plan. Guardrails cap order size and daily turnover and refuse buys outside the index.
  3. Agent: review_equity_order per order, then you confirm, then place_equity_order, then poll fills, then ddi record-fills.
  4. Refresh the snapshot, then ddi plan --phase buys and repeat. Run once a day mid-session. Fractional orders fill only in regular hours.

Rollout I'd suggest

  1. Run ddi mcp-tools to learn the real argument schemas and whether lot selection exists. Set lot_mode to match.
  2. Fund the MCP account with a small amount. Keep Trade approvals ON and run in review-only mode for a few weeks.
  3. Reconcile against Robinhood's realized P&L (get_realized_pnl) monthly.
  4. Only then scale up. Migrating from Wealthfront: transfer whole shares in-kind to keep basis, and don't let both systems trade the same names during the overlap.

Limitations

  • Survivorship. Yahoo has no prices for many removed or acquired members. 75% of Jan 2016 members are priceable, rising to 99% by 2026. The backtest universe skews toward survivors, which flatters returns and probably understates harvesting, since losers are missing.
  • Weights use SPY's current float-adjusted share counts held constant, and Yahoo counts for names no longer in the index. Buybacks and issuance are ignored. That's why tracking is reported against both a synthetic index from the same data and against real SPY.
  • Taxes are modelled. Losses are assumed fully usable: you need outside gains, and losses beyond $3k of ordinary income carry forward. Dividend taxes are ignored for both sides. Wash sales match on ticker only. State tax isn't modelled.
  • Execution. Fills at the close with 2 bps cost. Real fractional orders route to market makers. MCP rate limits and errors are unmodelled.
  • Untested against the live server. I did not connect to your Robinhood account. The headless client's argument names are guesses until ddi mcp-tools runs.
